#1 Nvidia's $500B AI Compute Securitization — Private Capital Becomes the Backbone of the Buildout ⚡ 92 STRUCTURAL / ASSET CLASS FORMATION

Summary: Nvidia has struck deals with Apollo, Blackstone, Goldman Sachs, and other Wall Street giants targeting $500B in AI infrastructure financing. The SEC has simultaneously issued an exemption clearing data-center asset-backed bonds. This is not a standard equity raise — it is an attempt to turn GPU compute capacity into a financeable, securitizable asset class, essentially what mortgages were to housing. Jensen Huang is solving AI's core constraint (capex) by offloading it to private credit markets.

Why interesting: This is a category-creating moment. If AI compute becomes a bond-market asset class, it unlocks a flywheel: more supply, lower cost of capital, higher utilization pressure on hyperscalers. It also creates systemic risk linkages that didn't exist before — private credit exposed to GPU utilization rates. The overbuilding concern flagged by Pitchbook is the key bear tension: you're securitizing an asset whose cash flows depend on demand for compute that may be overstated.

Key tension: Is this 'data centers are the new infrastructure' or 'CDOs backed by GPU clusters'? The difference matters enormously.

#3 SF Fed: Stock-Bond Correlation Has Flipped — We Are Now in a Structural Supply-Shock Regime ⚡ 82 MACRO REGIME / STRUCTURAL SIGNAL

Summary: The San Francisco Fed published a paper today formally documenting that the stock-bond correlation has flipped from positive to negative — the same structural shift last seen in the 1990s. Their interpretation: the dominant economic risk has moved from demand-side (secular stagnation, ZLB) to supply-side (energy shocks, AI-driven productivity disruption, tariffs, immigration). The stock-oil correlation has also changed sign. Oil uncertainty now correlates with HIGHER asset valuations (hedging demand), not lower.

Why interesting: This is not a commentary piece — this is the Fed's research arm formalizing a regime change. The practical implication: the traditional 60/40 portfolio hedging assumption is broken. Bonds no longer protect when stocks sell off (they both sell off together in a supply shock). Portfolio construction, risk parity strategies, and pension fund ALM frameworks built on positive stock-bond correlation are all structurally mishedged.

Key tension: Is this regime durable or a temporary artifact of the Hormuz shock? The 1990s parallel suggests supply-driven regimes can persist for years, not months.

Leads to investigate:
